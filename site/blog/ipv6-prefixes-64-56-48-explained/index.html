/64 vs /56 vs /48: How IPv6 Prefixes Affect Account Separation

November 10, 2026 · 2 min read

In short

An IPv6 /64 is one network, roughly one household or one server. Many platforms group addresses by /64, so proxies inside the same /64 can look related. A /56 holds 256 separate /64s and a /48 holds 65,536; with a routed /56 or /48 you can give every account its own /64.

IPv6 gives you so many addresses that "one IP per account" stops being the hard part. The real question becomes how many networks your accounts are spread over. That is what the number after the slash tells you.

What the slash means

An IPv6 address has 128 bits. A prefix like 2001:db8:51c0:1e::/64 says the first 64 bits identify the network and the remaining 64 bits are free for addresses inside it. The smaller the number after the slash, the bigger the block.

/64~18.4 quintillion addresses, one networkOne home LAN, one VPS
/56256 × /64Some home ISPs (via prefix delegation), some VPS providers
/4865,536 × /64Some VPS and dedicated server providers, businesses

Why a /64 is "one household"

By convention, a /64 is the smallest network handed to a single customer: your home router or your VPS. Every device in your house gets addresses from the same /64. Platforms know this, so their abuse, rate-limit and reputation systems often look at the /64, not the individual address.

In practice: twenty proxies with twenty different addresses in the same /64 are much better than twenty accounts on one shared IPv4, but a platform can still see that they come from the same network. For a handful of related accounts that is fine; for accounts that must look unrelated, it isn't.

Getting more /64s

  1. A VPS with a routed /56 or /48. The provider sends the whole prefix to your server. openv6 can then give each proxy its own random /64 inside it: tick "Each proxy in its own /64" when creating proxies on an anyip pool. See IPv6 proxies on a VPS.
  2. More locations. Each home connection and each VPS brings its own /64. openv6 manages them all as nodes in one dashboard, and the dashboard shows the number of distinct /64 subnets you are using.
  3. Prefix delegation at home. Some ISPs delegate a /56 or /60 to your router. Using those extra /64s for proxies depends on your router; ask your ISP what they provide.

Planning accounts across prefixes

  • Group by risk. Accounts that may share an owner (a brand and its backups) can share a /64. Accounts that must look independent should not.
  • Keep the mapping fixed. Once an account lives on a proxy, keep it there. Moving accounts between networks is a stronger signal than sharing one.
  • Residential vs datacenter. Home /64s are residential; VPS prefixes are datacenter. Platforms treat them differently; mix them on purpose, not by accident.
  • Don't forget IPv4. If a proxy falls back to IPv4 for IPv4-only sites, every such proxy shares the machine's IPv4, which undoes the separation. Keep fallback off on account proxies.

Quick math

/64 → 2^64 addresses          ≈ 18,446,744,073,709,551,616
/56 → 2^(64-56) = 2^8  /64s   = 256 networks
/48 → 2^(64-48) = 2^16 /64s   = 65,536 networks

Even a /56 gives more separate networks than most people will ever need for accounts; a single /64 gives more addresses than the entire IPv4 internet, many times over.

Frequently asked questions

How many /64 networks are in a /48?

65,536. A /56 contains 256 /64 networks, and each /64 contains 2^64 (about 18.4 quintillion) addresses.

Do websites block whole /64 ranges?

Rate limits, reputation and abuse systems often work per /64, because a /64 normally belongs to one customer. Treat a /64 like a single household when planning accounts.

How do I get more than one /64?

Use a VPS whose provider routes a /56 or /48 to the server, ask your ISP for prefix delegation, or combine several connections and servers, each with its own /64.

Does openv6 warn about shared /64s?

Yes. The dashboard shows how many distinct /64 networks your proxies use and warns when many accounts share one.
